PRP uses your body's own concentrated platelets to reduce inflammation and promote nerve fiber regeneration, and early research suggests it may provide longer-lasting relief than corticosteroid injections.

How Carpal Tunnel Syndrome Works

Carpal Tunnel Syndrome occurs when the median nerve, which runs through the wrist's carpal tunnel, becomes compressed. Common causes include:

Repetitive wrist movements (typing, assembly line work)

Wrist anatomy (smaller tunnel space)

Health conditions (diabetes, thyroid issues)

PRP for Carpal Tunnel: What the Research Says

Research into PRP for CTS is still emerging. Key points from recent studies:

Small clinical trials suggest reduced pain and improved nerve conduction velocity after PRP injections.

A 2020 randomized study found PRP provided longer-lasting relief than corticosteroid injections in mild to moderate CTS.

Animal models show PRP may support nerve fiber regeneration and reduce inflammation around the median nerve.

While results are encouraging, larger randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm:

Optimal PRP preparation methods

Ideal injection protocols (volume, frequency)

Long-term efficacy

Risks and Considerations

PRP is generally well-tolerated, but it's not risk-free:

Injection-site pain: Mild to moderate discomfort at the injection site for a few days.

Infection: Rare, but possible with any injection—practitioners use sterile techniques to minimize risk.

No guaranteed success: Not everyone experiences relief; some may need additional or alternative treatments.

Cost and accessibility: Insurance coverage varies; out-of-pocket expenses can be significant.

Before considering PRP, discuss potential benefits and risks with a qualified healthcare provider.

Who Might Consider PRP for Carpal Tunnel?

Ideal candidates for PRP therapy often include:

Individuals with mild to moderate CTS who haven't responded to splints or anti-inflammatories

Patients seeking a minimally invasive approach before considering surgery

Those interested in biologic therapies to enhance natural healing

PRP may be less effective for advanced CTS with significant nerve damage, where surgical decompression is often more reliable.

What to Expect During PRP Treatment

Initial Evaluation– Medical history review, physical exam, and often nerve conduction studies or ultrasound.

Blood Draw and PRP Preparation– Approximately 30–60 minutes to draw blood and spin it in a centrifuge.

Injection– Ultrasound guidance helps deliver PRP precisely around the median nerve. Local anesthesia may be used.

Post-Injection Care– Avoid heavy wrist use for 48–72 hours.– Gentle wrist movements and light activity encouraged afterward.– Follow-up visits to assess symptom improvement.

Most patients notice gradual relief over weeks as the growth factors work to reduce inflammation and encourage tissue repair.
