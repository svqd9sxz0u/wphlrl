Why is my pain worse? PRP injection side effects and medical next steps

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

PRP injections commonly cause increased pain, swelling, and stiffness within the first 48–72 hours. This happens because concentrated platelets trigger a controlled inflammatory response that promotes healing but temporarily irritates surrounding tissues.

Your recovery depends on several factors, including normal healing timelines, self-care strategies (rest, ice, and avoiding anti-inflammatory medications), and recognizing warning signs of complications such as fever, severe pain, or signs of infection. See below for complete details on side effects and when to seek medical attention.

Why Is My Pain Worse After a PRP Injection?Understanding PRP Injection Side Effects and Next Steps

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections are increasingly used to treat tendon, ligament, muscle and joint injuries. By concentrating your own platelets and growth factors, they aim to speed healing. However, it's common to experience more pain right after the injection. Here's what you need to know, in clear language, about why your pain may get worse, the typical PRP injection side effects, and what you can do next.

Why Pain Often Increases After PRPPRP works by triggering a mild, controlled inflammatory response. While inflammation is a key part of healing, it can also cause discomfort.

• Needle trauma: Any injection pierces tissue and may irritate nerves.• Platelet activation: When platelets release growth factors, they recruit immune cells and increase blood flow—both of which can hurt.• Swelling: Extra fluid in the tendon, joint or muscle can stretch sensitive tissues.• Local chemical changes: Growth factors and enzymes shift the pH and pressure around the injection site.

This "healing inflammation" can make your pain feel worse for several days before it improves.

Common PRP Injection Side EffectsMost side effects are mild to moderate and temporary. They include:

• Increased pain and stiffness at the injection site, peaking around 24–72 hours• Swelling or a "puffy" feeling in the treated area• Bruising from needle puncture or small blood vessel leaks• Redness and warmth around the injection spot• Mild fever or flu-like symptoms (rare)• Temporary stiffness in nearby joints

These effects typically resolve within a week to 10 days. If they last longer than two weeks or dramatically worsen, you should seek advice.

Expected Timeline of DiscomfortUnderstanding how discomfort normally evolves can ease your mind and guide self-care:

• First 48–72 hours: Peak pain, swelling and stiffness• Days 4–7: Gradual reduction in pain, though mild aching may persist• Week 2 onward: Slow transition from discomfort to a feeling of tightness or mild soreness• Weeks 4–6: You may begin gentle physical therapy or exercises as directed by your provider

If pain spikes again after initial improvement, or if you develop new symptoms (see Section 5), contact your healthcare provider.

Why Monitoring Matters: When to WorryMost side effects are part of the normal healing process. However, complications can occur. Watch for:

• Signs of infection: increasing redness, warmth, pus or a foul smell• Severe, throbbing pain that doesn't ease with rest or prescribed pain relief• Fever above 100.4°F (38°C)• Numbness, tingling or weakness around the injection site• Excessive swelling that hinders circulation (pale, cold fingers or toes)• Bleeding that soaks your bandage

Self-Care and Medical Next StepsHere's how to manage expected discomfort and when to escalate care:

Self-Care at Home• Rest: Limit strenuous activity for at least 48–72 hours.• Ice: Apply cold packs for 15–20 minutes every 2–3 hours to reduce swelling.• Compression: Use an elastic bandage or sleeve to control fluid buildup.• Elevation: Keep the treated limb raised above heart level when possible.• Pain relief: Acetaminophen (Tylenol) is preferred. Avoid NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) for 1–2 weeks unless your doctor approves, as they may blunt the PRP's healing effect.

Physical Therapy• Begin gentle range-of-motion exercises as advised by your provider.• Progress to strengthening exercises after 2–4 weeks, depending on your pain and tissue tolerance.• Consistency is key: follow your therapist's home program to support healing.

Follow-Up with Your Provider• Scheduled check-in: Most PRP clinics recommend a visit 4–6 weeks post-injection.• Imaging: Ultrasound or MRI may be ordered if pain persists beyond expected timelines.• Repeat injection: Some conditions benefit from a second or third PRP dose, spaced 4–6 weeks apart.• Alternative treatments: If PRP isn't effective, discuss options like corticosteroid injections, physical therapy intensification, shockwave therapy or, rarely, surgery.

When to Seek Immediate Medical AttentionContact emergency services or go to the nearest urgent care if you experience:

• Severe, sudden swelling or tightness that compromises breathing or circulation• Signs of acute infection with rapidly rising fever• Intense, unrelenting pain not improved by prescribed measures• Any symptom you feel could be life-threatening or serious

Otherwise, schedule a same-day or next-day appointment with your physician for concerning but non-emergent issues.

Why Communication MattersOpen dialogue with your healthcare team helps you get the best outcome:

• Report side effects honestly: Don't downplay your pain.• Ask about expected progress: Understand when and how you should feel better.• Clarify medication use: Confirm which pain relievers are safe without hindering PRP effectiveness.• Set realistic goals: PRP often works over weeks to months, not days.

Putting It All Together• A temporary increase in pain after a PRP injection is common—this signals the body's healing response.• Most side effects (swelling, bruising, stiffness) resolve in 7–10 days.• Use RICE (rest, ice, compression, elevation) and acetaminophen to manage symptoms.• Avoid NSAIDs unless directed by your doctor.• Monitor for signs of infection or severe complications and seek care promptly.• Stick with guided physical therapy to support tissue repair.• Keep in touch with your provider; discuss follow-up imaging or repeat PRP if needed.• If you're experiencing unusual symptoms and aren't sure whether they're normal, check your symptoms with Ubie's free AI tool for instant guidance before your next appointment.
