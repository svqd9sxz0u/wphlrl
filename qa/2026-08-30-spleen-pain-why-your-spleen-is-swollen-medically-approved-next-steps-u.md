Explanation

If you're feeling pain or pressure in the upper left side of your abdomen, your spleen could be involved. While spleen problems are not as common as other digestive issues, a swollen spleen (called splenomegaly) can signal an underlying health condition that deserves attention.

This guide explains what the spleen does, why spleen pain happens, what causes spleen swelling, and what medically approved next steps you should take.

What Does the Spleen Do?

The spleen is a fist-sized organ located in the upper left side of your abdomen, just under your rib cage and next to your stomach.

It plays several important roles:

Filters your blood by removing old or damaged red blood cells

Stores platelets (which help your blood clot)

Helps fight infections by producing and storing white blood cells

Supports your immune system

You can live without a spleen, but it plays a valuable role in keeping your body healthy.

What Does Spleen Pain Feel Like?

Spleen pain is usually felt:

In the upper left abdomen

Under the left rib cage

Sometimes spreading to the left shoulder

It may feel like:

A dull ache

A sense of fullness or pressure

Pain that worsens when taking a deep breath

Discomfort after eating small amounts (because a swollen spleen presses on the stomach)

In some cases, there may be no pain at all, and a swollen spleen is only discovered during a physical exam or imaging test.

What Does It Mean If Your Spleen Is Swollen?

A swollen spleen (splenomegaly) is not a disease itself. It is usually a sign of another medical condition.

When the spleen becomes enlarged, it may:

Trap too many blood cells

Reduce the number of healthy blood cells in circulation

Increase the risk of rupture if injured

A severely enlarged spleen can become fragile, which is why unexplained upper-left abdominal pain should never be ignored.

Common Causes of a Swollen Spleen

There are several medically recognized causes of spleen enlargement. These include:

1. Infections

Infections are one of the most common reasons the spleen swells.

Viral infections (such as mononucleosis)

Bacterial infections

Parasitic infections

Severe systemic infections

Infections cause the immune system to ramp up activity, which can enlarge the spleen.

2. Liver Disease

Conditions affecting the liver can impact blood flow through the spleen.

Cirrhosis

Chronic liver disease

Portal hypertension (increased pressure in the portal vein)

When blood flow backs up, the spleen can enlarge.

3. Blood Disorders

Certain blood-related conditions can cause spleen swelling:

Hemolytic anemia (rapid breakdown of red blood cells)

Leukemia

Lymphoma

Myeloproliferative disorders

These conditions may also cause fatigue, easy bruising, or frequent infections.

5. Cancers

When Is Spleen Pain an Emergency?

While many causes of spleen swelling are treatable, there are situations where urgent care is necessary.

Seek emergency medical attention if you experience:

Sudden, severe pain in the upper left abdomen

Pain that spreads to the left shoulder

Lightheadedness or fainting

Confusion

Rapid heart rate

Signs of internal bleeding

These symptoms could indicate a ruptured spleen, which is life-threatening and requires immediate care.

How Doctors Diagnose a Spleen Problem

If you visit a healthcare provider for suspected spleen pain, they may:

Perform a physical exam (feeling for enlargement)

Order blood tests to check for infection or blood disorders

Use ultrasound or CT imaging

Evaluate liver function

Review your medical history and recent infections

Early evaluation helps identify the root cause and guide treatment.

Medically Approved Next Steps

If you suspect spleen pain or swelling, here are safe and appropriate steps to take:

✅ 1. Pay Attention to Symptoms

Track:

Location and type of pain

Fever

Fatigue

Easy bruising

Unintended weight loss

Recent infections

This information helps your doctor make an accurate diagnosis.

✅ 2. Avoid Contact Sports

If your spleen is enlarged or suspected to be enlarged:

Avoid football, martial arts, or other high-impact activities

Can a Swollen Spleen Go Back to Normal?

Yes — in many cases.

If the underlying condition is treated effectively:

The spleen often returns to normal size

Pain resolves

Blood counts improve

For example, spleen enlargement from a viral infection like mononucleosis often improves over several weeks with rest and recovery.

Can You Prevent Spleen Problems?

You cannot prevent all causes of spleen swelling, but you can reduce risk by:

Staying up to date on vaccinations

Avoiding excessive alcohol use (protects liver health)

Seeking early treatment for infections

Managing chronic medical conditions

Wearing seatbelts to prevent abdominal trauma

The Bottom Line on Spleen Pain

The spleen is an important immune and blood-filtering organ. Pain or swelling in this area is usually a sign of another condition, such as infection, liver disease, or a blood disorder.

Most causes are treatable — but they require proper diagnosis.

Do not ignore:

Persistent upper-left abdominal pain

Unexplained fatigue

Sudden severe abdominal pain

If you are experiencing symptoms that could indicate a serious or life-threatening condition — especially severe pain or signs of internal bleeding — seek immediate emergency care.
