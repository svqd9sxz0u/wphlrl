Why Won't Your Bicep Heal? PRP for Bicep Tendonitis: Medically Approved Next Steps

Bicep tendonitis happens when the tendon connecting your biceps muscle to your shoulder or elbow becomes irritated. It can linger for months if not addressed properly. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy is gaining traction as a next-step treatment for stubborn cases. Here's what you need to know.

Why Bicep Tendonitis Sometimes Doesn't Heal

Tendonitis should improve with rest and physical therapy (PT), but chronic cases persist. Common reasons include:

Poor blood supply. Tendons have limited circulation, slowing healing.

Repetitive strain. Overhead activities (throwing, lifting) keep stressing the tendon.

Microscopic degeneration. Tiny tears and collagen breakdown accumulate over time.

Biomechanical issues. Impingement, muscle imbalances, or poor posture can perpetuate stress.

Inadequate rehab. Failing to follow through with a structured PT program or modifying activity too little.

Age and systemic factors. Aging, smoking, diabetes, or certain medications can impair tissue repair.

If you've tried rest, ice, anti-inflammatories, and PT for 6–12 weeks with little relief, it's reasonable to explore advanced options.

Diagnosing Chronic Bicep Tendonitis

A precise diagnosis guides appropriate treatment. Your doctor may use:

Physical exam. Special tests (Speed's, Yergason's) pinpoint biceps involvement.

Ultrasound. Real-time imaging reveals tendon thickening, tears, or fluid buildup.

MRI. Detailed views of tendon structure and nearby joint pathology (rotator cuff, labrum).

X-ray. Rules out bone spurs or arthritis that could aggravate the tendon.

Once confirmed, treatment can be tailored—PRP for bicep tendonitis is one such option.

Standard Treatments Before PRP

Before considering injections, ensure you've optimized conservative care:

Activity modification. Avoid overhead motions and heavy lifting.

Ice and NSAIDs. Short-term relief of pain and swelling.

Eccentric strengthening (slow lengthening contractions).

Scapular stabilization and posture correction.

Flexibility exercises for the shoulder girdle.

Corticosteroid injection. May reduce inflammation but offers limited tendon healing and potential side effects with repeated use.

Extracorporeal shockwave therapy. High-energy sound waves that stimulate blood flow and tissue repair.

If these measures fail after 3 months, PRP for bicep tendonitis becomes a valid consideration.

What Is PRP and How Does It Work?

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is a concentration of your own blood platelets and growth factors. The theory:

Platelet activation. Growth factors released from activated platelets encourage collagen synthesis and new blood vessel formation.

Tissue regeneration. Stimulated cells can accelerate tendon remodeling and repair.

Key points about PRP:

It's autologous (from your own blood), minimizing risk of allergic reaction.

It delivers 3–10 times the normal platelet concentration.

It's an orthobiologic approach—using body-derived substances to heal musculoskeletal injuries.

The PRP Injection Process

Blood draw. About 20–60 mL of your blood is collected.

Centrifugation. A machine spins the sample to separate red cells, plasma, and platelets.

Platelet concentration. The plasma layer rich in platelets is isolated.

Injection. Under ultrasound guidance, PRP is injected into and around the injured tendon.

Rest the shoulder/elbow for 24–48 hours.

Gradual return to gentle movement after 3–5 days.

Evidence for PRP in Bicep Tendonitis

Although research is still evolving, early studies and clinical experience suggest:

Improved pain scores. Many patients report significant pain reduction by 8–12 weeks post-injection.

Enhanced function. Range of motion and strength often recover beyond what is achieved with PT alone.

Reduced recurrence. PRP may lower the chance of tendonitis returning compared to steroid injections.

Keep in mind:

Results vary—some people experience dramatic relief, others modest gains.

Full benefits may take up to 6 months to manifest.

PRP is not universally covered by insurance, so costs can range from $500 to $2,000 per session.

Alternative or Adjunctive Treatments

If PRP alone is insufficient or not an option:

Tenotomy or tenodesis. Surgical procedures to remove damaged tendon tissue or reattach the biceps tendon.

Stem cell therapy. Still investigational; aims to add regenerative cells to the injury site.

Amniotic/umbilical tissue injections. Emerging biologics that may provide growth factors and anti-inflammatory proteins.

Platelet-rich fibrin (PRF). A next-generation platelet concentrate that includes fibrin matrix.

Discuss these alternatives with your physician if PRP results are suboptimal.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

While tendonitis itself isn't life threatening, certain symptoms warrant prompt evaluation:

Sudden, severe shoulder or elbow weakness.

Inability to lift your arm or bend your elbow at all.

Signs of infection after an injection: fever, increasing redness, or warmth at the site.

New-onset chest pain, shortness of breath, or other systemic concerns.
