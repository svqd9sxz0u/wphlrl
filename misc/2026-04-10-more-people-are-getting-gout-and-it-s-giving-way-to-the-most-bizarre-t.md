First, What is Gout and Why are Cases Rising?

The condition is a type of arthritis that’s caused by uric acid crystals building up in the joints and surrounding tissues, leading to severe pain and swelling. While the condition can happen to anyone at any age, it's mostly diagnosed in men than women, at around 60 to 64.

Gout currently impacts about 1 to 4 percent of people globally. That may not sound like much, but projections for 2050 forecasts the number of individuals with gout worldwide will increase by over 70 percent from 2020 to 2050.

Why would gout cases skyrocket from 4 to 70 percent? Scientists who made the forecast pointed it to a growing population that's becoming older and bigger. Obesity and high blood pressure are common risk factors for gout. Given the growing concern of gout, TikTokers have dispensed their own two cents on how to solve the problem.

How Much of TikTok's Gout Advice is Helpful?

When researchers combed through the first 200 videos on the #gout discover page, they found most advice was bogus.

About 38 percent of the videos tried to provide health advice, 20 percent talked about personal experiences with gout, and 19 percent were trying to promote or sell products. However, 79 percent of the reels overall talked about ways to manage gout.

These included what foods to avoid, though many promoted supplements, herbal products, or their personal home remedies. Many of these are “misleading or inconsistent with rheumatology guidelines,” the researchers wrote.

“With the rise of technology and social media use, gout information can be disseminated much wider and more rapidly than ever before,” adds Samuela Ofanoa, PhD, lead study author and research fellow at the University of Auckland in New Zealand. “While this can be beneficial if the information being spread is clinically proven, it can also be problematic if it’s misleading or not supported by clinical evidence.”

If gout content has found its way into your feed, Ofanoa recommends fact-checking what you see. Or you can keep reading to see what doctors think about the most common gout health advice.

4 Most Common Misconceptions on Gout

Sliced ginger root on joints helps with pain.

[People are Getting](https://neurocurrent.china-akan.com/)

One poster said her husband has had gout for years, and this hack helps with pain. Doctors aren’t so convinced.

“Ginger may have some anti-inflammatory properties—but this is when taken internally, not applied to joints,” says Richard C. Wender, MD, professor and chair of family medicine and community health at the University of Pennsylvania Perelman School of Medicine. “That would not be a good approach to acute or chronic gout.”

Kwasi Y. Kwaadu, DPM, chair of the department of podiatry at Northwell’s Westchester Region, agrees. “There’s no foundational medical evidence supporting the topical application of sliced ginger root for gout relief,” he says.

Kwaadu does acknowledge people can experience a placebo effect from believing a treatment would work. That said, he warns that relying solely on unproven remedies like topical ginger for gout can delay appropriate medical treatment.

Gout is a “rich man’s disease.”

That’s not exactly true, despite some video claims.

“Historically, people assumed that gout was caused by overindulgence in alcohol and rich foods. Items those from lower socioeconomic statuses could not afford,” explains Eric Ascher, DO, family medicine physician at Northwell’s Lenox Hill Hospital. “We know there is so much more to gout: genetics, kidney function, and complementary medical treatments.”

Still, Wender says there’s a small association between eating high amounts of red meat and developing gout. “To the extent that red meat intake was associated with having wealth, it’s pretty clear why this concept of it being a ‘rich man’s disease’ emerged,” he says.

Tart cherry juice lowers the risk of gout and helps manage it.

This isn't the first time Wender has heard of this remedy. He says the claim comes up a lot.

“Data supporting this is inconsistent at best,” Wender explains. “People should not rely on cherry juice for prevention or treatment.”

Ascher says tart cherry juice may help with some symptoms but it’s not a cure. “Cherries contain powerful antioxidants called anthocyanins, which reduce inflammation and oxidative stress in the body that is caused by uric acid crystals from a gout attack,” he points out.

If you do want to drink it, Ascher says to remember that tart cherry juice is a complimentary therapy, not an actual treatment.

You can treat gout with chicory and gardenia tea.

One video claims a certain chicory and gardenia tea blend can help with gout by balancing uric acid levels. (Keep in mind the poster makes a commission from people who purchase the product).

The claim isn’t true, according to Ascher. “There is no evidence that an herbal mix of chicory and gardenia tea can balance uric acid levels, treat, or prevent gout,” he says. “In fact, herbs have side effects and can interact with other medications.”
