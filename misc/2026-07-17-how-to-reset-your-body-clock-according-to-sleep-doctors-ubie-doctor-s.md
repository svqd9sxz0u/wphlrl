To reset your circadian rhythm, get bright morning light within 30 minutes of waking, keep a consistent sleep-wake schedule (even on weekends), and align meals, exercise, and a calming bedtime routine with your target sleep time. How to Reset Your Body Clock, According to Sleep Doctors

Resetting circadian rhythm—the internal clock that tells your body when to sleep and wake—is crucial for better sleep, mood, and overall health. Whether you've experienced jet lag, shift work, or simply drifted into late nights, sleep doctors recommend practical, science-backed strategies to help you reclaim a healthy schedule.

1. Understand Your Circadian Rhythm

Your circadian rhythm is a roughly 24-hour cycle driven by a "master clock" in the brain's suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN). This clock:

Responds primarily to light and darkness.

Regulates hormone release (like melatonin), body temperature, and alertness.

Can shift forward or backward based on environmental cues and lifestyle habits.

When your rhythm is misaligned—say, sleeping at 2 a.m. instead of 10 p.m.—you may feel groggy, irritable, or even develop longer-term health issues.

2. Use Light to Your Advantage

Light is the strongest cue for resetting circadian rhythm.

Morning Light Exposure

Aim to get 20–30 minutes of natural sunlight within an hour of waking.

Take a walk outside or have coffee by a bright window.

If natural light is limited, consider a light therapy box (10,000 lux) for 20–30 minutes.

Evening Light Reduction

Dim overhead lights two hours before bedtime.

Avoid screens (phones, tablets, TVs) or use blue-light–filtering settings/apps.

Use low-wattage bulbs or salt lamps to cue your brain that bedtime is near.

3. Keep a Consistent Sleep–Wake Schedule

Consistency anchors your circadian rhythm, even on weekends.

Choose a wake-up time you can stick with every day.

Calculate your bedtime based on at least 7–9 hours of sleep.

If you must change your schedule (e.g., after travel), shift by 15–30 minutes each day rather than making a big jump.

4. Mind Your Meals and Caffeine

The timing and content of what you eat and drink can influence your internal clock.

Meal Timing

Eat main meals at roughly the same times each day.

Avoid heavy meals within 2–3 hours of bedtime.

Research shows that front-loading calories (larger breakfast, smaller dinner) can help strengthen your rhythm.

Caffeine and Alcohol

Limit caffeine after midday; it can stay in your system up to 8 hours.

Avoid alcohol close to bedtime—it may help you fall asleep but fragments sleep later in the night.

5. Strategically Use Melatonin

Melatonin is a hormone your brain produces to signal sleep.

For most adults, 0.5–3 mg of melatonin 1–2 hours before your desired bedtime can help advance or delay sleep.

Timing is key: to shift earlier, take it in the late afternoon/early evening. To shift later (night owls), take it closer to bedtime.

Always consult your doctor before starting melatonin, especially if you take other medications.

6. Incorporate Regular Exercise

Physical activity is a powerful non-light way to shift your clock.

Morning exercise tends to advance the clock (earlier sleep onset), while late-afternoon workouts can delay it.

Aim for 20–60 minutes of moderate aerobic activity at least 3–4 times per week.

Avoid vigorous exercise within 1–2 hours of bedtime to prevent unwanted stimulation.

7. Create a Calming Bedtime Routine

A predictable pre-sleep ritual tells your brain it's time to wind down.

Pick 3–5 relaxing activities (e.g., reading a book, gentle yoga, meditation, warm bath).

Stick to the same sequence and timing each night.

Keep the environment cool (around 65°F/18°C), dark, and quiet.

If travel or shift changes are unavoidable, plan ahead.

Jet Lag Tips

Eastward travel (losing time): seek morning light, avoid late-afternoon light.

Westward travel (gaining time): seek late-afternoon light, avoid early-morning light.

Sync meal times and sleep as soon as possible to the new time zone.

Shift Work Strategies

Use bright light at the start of your shift and wear sunglasses commuting home.

Create a dark, quiet sleeping environment during the day.

Keep a consistent "anchor" sleep period (even on days off) to reduce swings.

9. Watch for Common Pitfalls

Napping too long or too late can undo progress.

Inconsistent schedules (bedtime at 10 p.m. one night, 1 a.m. the next) confuse your clock.

Overreliance on screens or bright lights after sunset delays melatonin release.

10. When to Seek Professional Help

If you've tried these steps consistently for 2–4 weeks and still struggle with sleep timing or quality, consider consulting a sleep specialist. Chronic circadian disorders—like delayed sleep-phase syndrome—often require tailored behavioral therapy or short-term use of prescription medications.

If you're experiencing persistent sleep issues alongside other symptoms, it may be helpful to check your symptoms with a free AI-powered tool to better understand what might be affecting your rest and whether you should seek professional evaluation.

Final Thoughts

Resetting your body clock takes patience and consistency. By leveraging light, routine, and healthy habits, you can realign your internal schedule and enjoy more restorative sleep. If you experience severe daytime sleepiness, ongoing insomnia, or any alarming symptoms, speak to a doctor promptly—your health and safety come first.
