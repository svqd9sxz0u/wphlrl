# Inside the Billion-Dollar 'Make America Healthy Again' Boom

ON A JUNE afternoon in Venice, California, nearly a thousand Angelenos lined up across two city blocks. They weren’t clamoring for a peek at Arnold working out at Muscle Beach or a Supreme merch drop, but perhaps something just as notable in the year of our Lord 2025: beef tallow French fries. They had come from all over L.A. to eat seed oil–free food with Paul Saladino, MD, the animal-based-diet guy formerly known as @carnivoremd, who was making a guest appearance at Talo Organic.

The fast-casual counter restaurant leans into its differentiators: namely, pasture-raised proteins prepared with organic ingredients, nontoxic cookware, reverse osmosis water, and, of course, zero seed oils (cooking oils extracted from the seeds of various plants, such as canola oil, safflower oil, and grapeseed oil). At the event, Talo Organic founder Graham Honig wore a T-shirt emblazoned with the motto seed oils suck. (Talo Organic has sold a few hundred of the signature tees.) “The lobby was so flooded, it spilled out onto the streets,” Honig says. All kinds of influencers started showing up, eager to share their reviews and take photos of the area’s hottest new health restaurant.

It was a “full-circle moment” for Honig, 28, a longtime fan of Dr. Saladino, who believes seed oils “strongly contribute to increased cardiovascular disease, and, in the long term, obesity.” (Expert dietitians and decades of scientific research state otherwise.) In fact, Dr. Saladino was one of the inspirations behind the restaurant idea. Just eight months prior, Honig had left a finance job and—despite having no restaurant experience—launched a weekend pop-up grill in his native Sacramento: a small, modest tent selling organic and seed oil–free rice bowls, salads, tacos, and fries. And they were selling out. “We had a few moms that drove an hour and a half to come meet us,” Honig recalls. “They were almost in tears, saying, ‘Thank you…we haven’t been able to eat out in years.’ ”

Talo’s Instagram exploded that winter, growing from 200 followers to 50,000 in just three months. In March, Honig closed a seven-figure funding round, and by May, Talo Organic was setting up shop with a counter space in Venice. Among his investors are tech entrepreneur Vinny Lingham (who produced a documentary on the carnivore diet), NBA player Spencer Dinwiddie, MLB player Noah Syndergaard, and White House health advisor Calley Means (brother of surgeon general nominee Casey Means, MD).

Talo Organic is the latest manifestation of consumers seeking “better for you” ingredients, spurring countless restaurants, snacks, grooming products, and even toilet paper. But the trend has gained new momentum with the Make America Healthy Again (MAHA) movement, led by U.S. Department of Health and Human Services secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who has stated that Americans are being “unknowingly poisoned” by seed oils (a claim not shared by health organizations such as the American Heart Association), that “sugar is poison” (no, poison is poison), that fluoride in drinking water is “an industrial waste” (it has also protected the oral health of Americans for decades), and that artificial food dyes are “poisonous compounds” (take that, M&Ms!).

Much like the term wellness, MAHA is an ambiguous shorthand without strict borders or definition, encompassing a wide range of trends and concerns, all in the hope of addressing high rates of chronic disease. Rather than declaring they’re a part of MAHA, people will often say that RFK Jr. legitimizes their long-held beliefs and values—but the telltale signs are as evident as the circles on a cupping patient: avoiding seed oils, synthetic food dyes, and ultra-processed foods; checking food toxicity apps; and expressing frustration over “Big Pharma” and “Big Food.”

This latest interest in what we put in and on our bodies has translated into big bucks for some companies. The surge in demand for certified seed oil–free products, for example, resulted in year-over-year sales growth of 216 percent from 2024 to 2025, according to SPINS Product Intelligence, while sales of raw milk increased by nearly 18 percent in 2024, per the market research firm Circana. This is feeding what we’re calling a “MAHAconomy”—MAHA-friendly products and services from emerging and established businesses that aim to profit off the MAHA agenda.

Granted, it’s impossible to put a value on this MAHAconomy because many of the movement’s trends either already existed or are so new that they haven’t yet been analyzed. “It’s hard to say whether [RFK Jr.] bringing seed oils into mainstream conversation actually accelerated our growth, or if we’re just seeing the momentum of an already growing, consumer-driven health movement,” says Jonathan Rubin, CEO and founder of the Seed Oil Free Alliance, a certification organization that has approved over 200 products, from hummus to potato chips. But even if the exact numbers are fuzzy, it’s clear that MAHA is impacting government policy and the consumer product space, with entrepreneurs and conglomerates vying for a piece of the organic pie.

Elly Truesdell, founder of New Fare Partners, a venture capital fund centered on healthier foods and beverages, says trends like avoiding seed oils are indeed having a real industry effect, with “a ton of companies seizing on” anti–seed oil anxiety (see: Sweetgreen). Brands that already use avocado or coconut oil—i.e., seed oil alternatives—are also attracting consumers, “and it’s absolutely translating to growth and sales,” says Truesdell. Honig, who says 90 percent of his customers cite seed oil avoidance (or being “tallow-based,” a fashionable way to say “beef fat appreciator”) as their reason for choosing Talo Organic, agrees: “It has exploded.”

As health becomes more and more like fashion, every season ushers in some new ingredient or fad, along with new versions of science-washing that make it increasingly hard to separate the wheat from the gluten-free chaff. But will these trends have a lasting impact, or will they sour faster than raw milk?

A Counter Culture Made Anew

IF BONE BROTH and kombucha once swayed the conscientious shopper, now raw milk and emulsifier-free salad dressings fill up shopping carts and social media feeds. This “natural” food obsession isn’t actually new—it has roots in the countercultural natural food movement of the 1970s. Spurred by the publication of books like Rachel Carson’s Silent Spring, which documented the potential environmental and human harms of pesticides, the movement brought greater awareness to synthetic chemicals and, overall, to what we eat and how it’s farmed.

Some Americans rallied against industrialized agriculture, a backlash against the overprocessing of, well, everything. “Natural,” local, and organic became fashionable for a certain sect, many of whom were associated with the hippie movement. That thread weaved through the ’90s, when brands like Tom’s of Maine and Amy’s Kitchen took off, and experienced another surge in 2014 when people like food blogger Vani Hari led the charge against Starbucks and Subway menus. A decade later, we’re here again.

Farmer Mark McAfee was early to the scene. In 1999, he arrived in Venice Beach with 150 half-gallons of raw milk, expecting just modest interest. Instead, he was welcomed by over a hundred locals yelling, clapping, and shouting “thank you” as they stuffed $20 bills into his car. He sold out on the spot.

Today, McAfee is the CEO and founder of the world’s largest organic raw dairy brand, Raw Farm, which supplies more than 500 stores—with sales of over $30 million and growing as the company expands into raw cheese, butter, and kefir. (The farm also happens to be linked to one of the largest salmonella outbreaks from raw milk in recent history; scientists and the CDC caution against consuming unpasteurized dairy). RFK Jr., who once claimed that raw milk ​​“advances human health,” is one of his longtime customers. According to McAfee, who also leads the Raw Milk Institute, the health secretary asked the farmer to develop a new set of raw milk standards and guidance for the FDA. He did, in December 2024, but has yet to hear back. (“It’s much easier to slash budgets and fire 20,000 [employees] at the FDA, CDC, and NIH than it is to constructively put together a budget and create new things like standards for raw milk,” he grumbles.)

Overall, McAfee sees regulatory hesitance regarding raw milk more sinisterly, claiming that the “culture of the FDA is all about drugs…and do not ever mention food as a healing thing.” In this sense, MAHA has gathered an unlikely coalition of crunchy moms and raw-meat podcast bros who distrust (or simply dislike) the government. McAfee witnessed a paradigm shift after the COVID-19 pandemic, when Purell-weary Americans Googled how to strengthen their immune systems and, as he sees it, were awakened to the heavily debated idea that “food is medicine,” thereby trusting influencers and farmers over the FDA and pharmaceuticals.

This is a group that often coalesced around dissatisfaction—repulsion over the snack aisle, anger over pizza-laden school lunches, and disappointment with the regulatory forces they think should better safeguard what ends up on our dinner plate. Dissatisfaction is a potent fuel for movements, and this one is as focused on what it wants (farm-fresh produce, psychedelics, bacteria-packed dairy) as it is on what it doesn’t (artificial ingredients, corn syrup, birth control).

These are no longer fringe sentiments reserved for the hardcore wellness influencer—companies increasingly see widespread opportunities. The skin care brand Primally Pure, which sells facial sunscreen made with grass-fed tallow and “ancestral practices,” boasts billboards in prime Los Angeles locales—alongside ads for Marvel movies—that read the sun isn’t poison, but your sunscreen is. (Some sunscreens may contain potentially endocrine-disrupting chemicals, but they are generally considered to be safe.) Dough Drop Pizza, an “underground” L.A.-based pizza delivery service that has a four-month, 3,000-person wait list, sells $35 pizza pies that are completely free of seed oils, sugars, enriched flour, and commercial yeast.

One difference today, however, is that this is not just a consumer-driven initiative, pushed by petition-signing moms and wellness influencers. Some of the highest government officials and consultants are leading the way. Now, says Leah McGrath, RDN, a corporate dietitian for the supermarket chain Ingles Markets, “you have the political motivation.”

The MAHAconomy

THE HEALTH TECH and food industries are benefiting the most so far from the MAHA business boom. In April, RFK Jr. endorsed Yuka, a mobile app that scans food and personal care products for their purported health impact—based on nutritional value, additives, and organic ingredients—propelling it to number two on the list of health and fitness apps on the U.S. Apple App Store. Founded in 2017, Yuka was already gaining traction with people who wanted to know whether their deodorant, as their TikTok feeds claim, is indeed trying to kill them. In 2020, the app had 15 million users. Now it’s soared past 70 million, with 1 million of them paying subscribers for its premium version.

Some within RFK Jr.’s circle are even seeing their businesses tied to MAHA, and they’re reaping the benefits. A month after the Yuka endorsement, RFK Jr. announced that his department will launch a campaign to encourage every single American to wear a health tracker to “take responsibility” for their health. Surgeon general nominee Casey Means, MD, is the cofounder and chief medical officer of the metabolic health company Levels, which gives people without diabetes the ability to track their blood sugar in real time via a wearable continuous glucose monitor. Founded in 2019, Levels now has more than 80,000 paid members (subscriptions start at $199 a month). Dr. Means’s brother, Calley, who advises RFK Jr., has also garnered attention for his company, Truemed, which allows people to use their health savings accounts and flexible spending accounts for things like gym memberships, wearables, supplements, and saunas.

Mark Hyman, MD, another close friend of RFK Jr., is a Levels advisor and the co-founder of Function Health, a start-up offering a $499 yearly membership that includes access to over 160 blood and urine tests and will recommend supplements. In May, Dr. Hyman joined six other health tech leaders for a meeting with RFK Jr., who championed apps and other tools that, in his view, “empower Americans to take control of their health.” As of December 2024, more than 100,000 people had signed up for Function’s service, per a New York Times article, and the company is reportedly valued at $2 billion.

Food, however, remains the biggest MAHA growth contender. This is a consumer base that increasingly prefers natural, simpler ingredients. The push for more whole foods has gained traction, appealing to people’s nostalgia for a romanticized, “safer” food era that never really was. It’s also, frankly, a rebellion against the “alts”: The bland almond milk and faux-meat burgers that once had Silicon Valley in an investment tizzy have fizzled out for a number of reasons, including high prices, disappointing taste, and consumers’ preference for less-processed foods, including protein.

The protein craze drove growth in meat, fish, and poultry by 13 percent in 2024, compared with 5.8 percent in 2023, according to Nutrition Business Journal. Some of the fixation has taken exotic forms, such as the newly energized appreciation for organ meats. (Steak? How normie.) On Instagram, Primally Pure’s founder, Bethany McDaniel—who has hosted Dr. Means on her podcast, Grounded Wellness—shared her organ meat smoothie recipe, which includes, naturally, beef liver, raw milk, raw egg yolks, frozen berries, and a handful of mint leaves “for covering up the livery taste.”

Likewise, full-fledged dairy is back to being an industry darling, and organic all the more so. Organic dairy and egg sales grew 7.7 percent in 2024, while poor plant-based alt milk consumption fell for the third consecutive year. Milk is so back that we now have super milk: Fairlife, a milk made with 50 percent more protein and 50 percent less sugar, has seen sales jump by nearly 30 percent in the last year.

MAHA is already enjoying some big industry victories, and a bit of countercultural edginess. Food manufacturers like Kraft Heinz, Nestlé USA, General Mills, PepsiCo, and J.M. Smucker have already agreed to phase out artificial coloring and/or flavoring, handing a win to RFK Jr.’s pet project. Retailers are establishing more MAHA-approved divisions, Target has added a “no artificial colors” search filter, and brands are reformulating their products and packaging to reflect their “free from” evolution. We are headed to a future, says McGrath, in which a grocery store shelf is like “a miniature version of a NASCAR [race car], with labels all over food products.”

Except eliminating dyes isn’t a simple process (a complete phaseout might not occur until the end of the current administration), nor does it necessarily help the bottom line. Will the people who are concerned about food dyes still be willing to buy sugary cereals and snacks—like sand-hued Twinkies—or are companies potentially alienating their core consumers, who love the familiar tastes and colors? In 2016, General Mills swapped the artificial colors in its Trix cereal for natural dyes, like turmeric and radish juice, only to bring back the artificial version a year later after customers, raised on Crayola-colored cereals, complained that the new “natural” color was dull and tasted different. So, once we’ve reduced all snacks to the greige rainbow and the MAHA figureheads no longer have White House security clearances, will these trends still move product?

“Brands have to be very careful to figure out who their audience is,” cautions McGrath, noting that there’s a significant cost to making these pronouncements and changes. Among manufacturers, “there’s a big middle who are like, ‘Let’s see how this plays out.’ ”

A Lasting Boom (or Bust)?

DEMAND DOESN’T ALWAYS translate into profit, and many of these MAHA trends are as costly as home-cooked dinners are time-consuming. McAfee says that his raw milk is “much more expensive” to produce, while McGrath notes that seed oils often cost less and don’t go rancid as quickly. Honig adds that pure beef tallow can cost five times as much as soybean or canola oil, while some organic produce is double the cost of nonorganic. When New Fare Partners’ Truesdell invests in a brand, she needs to ensure that “it works with a Whole Foods customer, but also a Target, Walmart, and Costco shopper.” As you can imagine, few of these products cross over into mass retail.

Efficacy is another sticking point. “Clean,” a sector of the personal care market that experienced a surge in shelf space circa 2017, has recently seen a sales decline as consumers were potentially paying higher prices for less stable products. Some “natural” products may make claims about their effectiveness that are not supported by clinical evidence. Consumers might buy it once, but if a product fails to perform, they may revert to their old shopping habits. Fool me once, natural deodorant, shame on you. Fool me twice, shame on stinky me.

While analysts contemplate shifting consumer demands, some health experts wonder whether Americans might eventually realize that many MAHA initiatives are unlikely to have a significant impact on public health. Nutrition, for example, depends far more on a person’s overall eating habits than on their artificial dye intake. The average person’s exposure to pesticide residue, meanwhile, is infinitesimal: A 150-pound man would have to eat about 8,065 nectarines in one day before experiencing any potential ill effects from pesticides. And whether Coca-Cola uses high-fructose corn syrup or cane sugar (“You’ll see. It’s just better!” President Trump promised of the latter on Truth Social) may not matter all that much: They have a pretty similar effect on blood sugar levels.

Three-quarters of consumers might say they look for foods that are minimally processed, but what ends up in the fridge, often due to taste, affordability, and convenience, is another matter. The U.S. processed food market is projected to grow from $204 billion in 2025 to over $395 billion by 2034, with ultra-processed foods expected to grow fastest, according to a market report. People with the means to buy organic or an expensive gadget not covered by insurance are likely already eating a relatively nutritious diet and leading a healthy lifestyle. It’s precisely those who could use help in boosting their nutrition who encounter barriers to accessing many of these pricey products and whose health will see little, if any, impact from revamped Skittles.

Truly changing the U.S. food environment and Americans’ eating habits is a far more complex goal, necessitating ambitious, multifaceted policies that aim to eliminate food deserts, provide access to registered dietitians, and strengthen programs like the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program. “There’s lots of noise being made about small things like food additives that are not really getting at the major nutrition problems facing society,” says Kevin Klatt, PhD, a registered dietitian and research scientist at the University of California, Berkeley. “We’ve seen no real policies put into place that even remotely address everything from what we produce to how it ends up on our plates.”

As those policies are debated, we’ll likely see more niche brands and products, if only because the U.S. consumer wellness market is currently valued at over $500 billion, per McKinsey & Company, and 84 percent of U.S. consumers consider wellness a priority in their lives. Americans of a certain income and mindset will continue to express their sentiments and frustrations through their purchases.

“We have this huge health wave behind us,” says Honig, the Talo Organic founder, who has plans to expand to multiple cities. “We have people in every city across America begging us, ‘I need you to come to our town.’ ” And while he doesn’t officially identify with MAHA (“I don’t really give a crap about the politics”), Honig certainly appreciates what the MAHA movement is doing—“bringing sunlight to the issues,” as he puts it—and that undoubtedly sustains a steady business. “We’re going to try and raise a second round of funding,” he says. Perhaps he should bring a pitch deck to the next MAHA Commission meeting.
