Low Libido in Women: What Doctors Evaluate Beyond Hormone Levels

Low sexual desire (low libido) is common among women of all ages. While hormone levels are often the first thing doctors check, a thorough evaluation looks at many other factors. Understanding these can help you and your doctor find the right path to renewed interest in intimacy—without unnecessary anxiety.

Comprehensive Medical HistoryBefore ordering any tests, your doctor will explore your overall health. Key topics include:

Current and past medical conditions (diabetes, thyroid disease, cancer treatment)

Medications and supplements (antidepressants, blood pressure drugs)

Surgical history (hysterectomy, pelvic procedures)

Menstrual and menopausal status

This background helps identify conditions or treatments that can dampen sexual desire.

Stress levels (work, family, finances)

Couples counseling or sex therapy may help address relational barriers to intimacy.

Medications and Substance UseMany medications and substances can reduce libido. Your doctor will review:

Antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs)

Antihypertensives (beta-blockers, diuretics)

Hormonal contraceptives

Alcohol, tobacco, recreational drugs

Adjusting doses, switching drugs, or adding "add-back" treatments may restore desire.

Lifestyle and Sleep HabitsEveryday habits strongly influence sexual drive. Your doctor may explore:

Exercise routine (both too little and too much can affect hormones)

Diet and weight changes

Quality and quantity of sleep

Work hours and shift patterns

Improving sleep hygiene, reducing stress, and moderate exercise often boost energy and libido.

Chronic Illnesses and PainConditions causing ongoing pain or fatigue often lower desire. These include:

Arthritis or joint pain

Fibromyalgia or chronic fatigue syndrome

Pelvic pain disorders (endometriosis, interstitial cystitis)

Cardiovascular or respiratory disease

Effective pain management and fatigue reduction strategies can improve sexual interest.

Neurological and Circulatory FactorsNerve or blood flow issues can interfere with sexual response and desire. Assessment may cover:

Diabetic neuropathy

Multiple sclerosis or neurodegenerative diseases

Vascular health (hypertension, atherosclerosis)

Addressing these underlying problems often enhances both physical response and desire.

Sexual Pain and DysfunctionPain with sex (dyspareunia) or difficulty with arousal can feed back into low desire. Your doctor will ask about:

Pain during or after intercourse

Vaginal dryness or tightness

Difficulty becoming aroused or achieving orgasm

Treatments such as lubricants, dilator therapy, pelvic floor physical therapy or topical estrogen can make sex more comfortable and enjoyable.

What To Expect in a Doctor's VisitYour doctor's evaluation will usually include:

Detailed interview covering the factors above

Physical exam focusing on pelvic, breast and neurological health

Targeted lab tests beyond basic hormone panels (e.g., thyroid function, blood sugar, vitamin D)

Optional imaging (ultrasound or MRI) if structural problems are suspected

Referral to specialists (endocrinologist, gynecologist, urologist, mental health professional) as needed
